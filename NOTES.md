## Design notes

These are the layout decisions behind `entry.png`, the 1024x1024 broadsheet
poster that compresses Thursday 19 November 2026 onto a single page. The page
carries the day as a set of 38 stories spanning every region and field the
news touched - politics, courts, markets, science, quakes and sport - and
each decision documented below, from the paper colour to the date-check
footer, exists to keep those 38 stories reading as one coherent front page
rather than a pile of unrelated headlines. What follows is the reasoning
behind each choice, and what the choice does for a reader meeting the poster
for the first time.

**Why cream paper.** The page is printed on a warm off-white rather than pure
white because that is the colour newsprint actually ages into, and the goal is
for the poster to read as a physical front page rather than a web dashboard.
Cream also softens the contrast so dense columns of black text sit comfortably
instead of vibrating, and it lets the two accent colours on the page - the red
date tab and the black rules - carry all of the contrast, which keeps the page
feeling editorial rather than designed-by-palette.

**Why the red date tab.** A broadsheet answers two questions before any story:
who am I, and what day is this. The masthead answers the first, and the solid
red tab answers the second by making the date the single loudest element on the
page. Red is the only saturated colour used for furniture, so the eye lands on
it first and the poster is timestamped at a glance even at thumbnail size -
which matters for a contest graded as one image summarising one specific day.

**Why an eight-cell numbers strip.** Eight cells sit directly under the
masthead as a scanning row: eight figures is enough to cover the day's
politics, courts, markets, science, quakes and sport without the strip
becoming a second page of its own. A reader who looks at nothing else still
leaves with the day's magnitudes, and the equal sizing of the cells states
that no single number owns the day - the strip is a summary instrument, not a
leaderboard.

**How stories were ranked.** Ranking was driven by reach and by breadth
across regions and fields. The three hero cards went to the stories with the
widest global consequence, each given a large card so the page has a clear
pecking order at arm's length. The five secondary cards took major stories
that mattered but did not move the whole planet, and the seven tertiary cards
carried solid, regionally important items in a tighter type size. The
twenty-four evening briefs then swept up everything else as short
region-tagged lines - real events that belong on the record but do not need a
card. The result is a pyramid: three heroes read as the day's lead, the cards
fill the middle, and the briefs guarantee no region of the world goes
unrepresented.

**Why the date-check footer exists.** News cycles carry over: a story that
peaked on the 17th is still in the copy on the 19th. The footer exists to
draw that line explicitly, separating events that actually happened on
Thursday 19 November 2026 from carryover context, so the poster stays an
honest record of one day rather than a general "latest news" collage. It is
the page's own fact-check, and it is what makes the single-date promise of
the masthead credible.
